These concepts are nested — each inner level is a subset of the one outside it. Phonological awareness spans a continuum from least to most complex (Chard & Dickson, 1999): rhyming and sentence segmentation → syllables → onset–rime → phonemes. Phonemic awareness is the phoneme-level subset of phonological awareness. Phonics sits alongside (not inside) phonological awareness: it adds print to what is otherwise a purely auditory skill set.

Nested model. Each inner layer is a subset of the one outside it. Phonics and Letter–Sound Knowledge sit alongside — they require phonemic awareness plus print.

Phonological Awareness
Ears only — no letters

Awareness of the sound structure of spoken language across multiple grain sizes. Skills develop in overlapping phases from least to most complex (Chard & Dickson, 1999). Rhyming and sentence segmentation are the entry point — not an afterthought.

Rhyme & alliteration — least complex; word-level onset awareness
Sentence segmentation — words within sentences (precursor)
Syllable level — segment, blend, count, delete
Onset–rime — “cat” → /k/ + /æt/
Phoneme level (= phonemic awareness) — finest grain; literacy driver
Letter–Sound Knowledge
Visual + auditory

Knowing “b” represents /b/. A sub-skill of phonics, not a component of phonological awareness. Best taught alongside phonemic awareness (Ehri et al. 2001).

Phonics
Print + sound

Maps graphemes ↔ phonemes. Requires phonemic awareness plus letter–sound knowledge.

Decoding (print → sound)  ·  Encoding / spelling (sound → print)

Key distinction: Phonological and phonemic awareness are ears only — no letters. The moment a letter card appears, you’ve moved into letter–sound or phonics territory. Rhyming and alliteration are the least complex end of phonological awareness — they belong inside the PA umbrella, not outside it (Chard & Dickson, 1999). While PA is definitionally auditory, integrating letters once letter learning is underway produces stronger literacy outcomes than either in isolation (Ehri et al. 2001; Boyer & Ehri 2011).

What is this section about?
There are several related terms you might hear from your child’s teacher or speech pathologist. This section explains what they mean and how they fit together — think of them like Russian dolls, one inside the other.

Think of it like Russian dolls. Each inner skill is more detailed than the one outside it.

Sound Awareness
Listening only — no reading

Your child’s ability to hear and play with the sounds in spoken words — before they learn to read. This is a purely listening skill. No letters involved. It starts simple and builds in complexity.

Rhyming — “cat” and “hat” sound the same at the end
Alliteration — “ball, bat, bin” all start with /b/
Clapping syllables in “but-ter-fly”
Hearing sounds in “dog” → /d/ /o/ /g/
Letter–Sound Knowledge
Linking sounds to letters

Knowing that the letter “b” makes the sound /b/, that “sh” makes /sh/, and so on. A separate skill from sound awareness — both need to develop.

Phonics
Using letters to read and spell

When your child uses their sound awareness AND letter knowledge together to read and spell words. This is what happens in early reading lessons.

The key thing to know: Sound awareness is a listening skill that develops before — and supports — reading. A child who struggles to hear the individual sounds in words will often find learning to read much harder. The good news is that these skills can be directly taught and practised.

Phoneme isolation
UK: 4;6–4;11AU: 5;0–5;5
“What’s the first sound in ‘sun’?” → /s/. Entry point to phonemic awareness work. AU norm ~6–7 months later — attributable to later school entry.
Dodd & Gillon (2001) Table 2; PIPA normative data
6
Phoneme identity & categorisation ~age 5–6
Identity: “What sound is the same in bat, ball, big?” → /b/
Categorisation: “bat, ball, cat — which doesn’t fit?” → cat. Distinct tasks, frequently conflated (Yopp 1988; Gillon 2018).
7
Phoneme blending ~age 5–6
“What word is /d/–/ɔ/–/g/?” → “dog”. Generally easier than segmentation. Teach blending and segmentation integrated (NRP 2000). Connected phonation (/dddɔɔɔggg/) more effective than segmented for CVC decoding (Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri 2020).
8
Phoneme segmentation
UK: 5;6–5;11AU: 6;6–6;11
The last PA skill established. “Say each sound in ‘ship’” → /ʃ/–/ɪ/–/p/ (3 phonemes, not 4 letters). AU children establish this a full year later than UK. Underpins spelling. Use Elkonin sound boxes.
Dodd & Gillon (2001) Table 2; PIPA normative data
9
Phoneme deletion ~age 6–7
“Say ‘clap’ without /k/” → “lap”. Syllable-level deletion develops before phoneme deletion.
10
Phoneme substitution & manipulation ~age 6–8
Hardest tasks. “Change /b/ in ‘bat’ to /h/” → “hat”. Manipulation tasks correlate most strongly with reading ability (Kilpatrick 2012, 2015; see also Catts et al. 2001).
Clinical tip: Blending is generally easier — a useful starting point — but integrated blending and segmentation produces the greatest literacy benefit (NRP 2000). When a child can do syllable tasks but fails phoneme tasks, begin with phoneme isolation at word-initial position.

When do children learn these skills?
These skills develop gradually over the preschool and early school years. Every child is different, but there are general milestones to watch for. In Australia, children start school at age 5 — one year later than the UK — so Australian milestones are slightly later too.
Note for Australian families: The ages below reflect what is typical for Australian children. You may see slightly earlier ages in American or British resources — this reflects different school starting ages, not a problem with your child.
Does my child speak more than one language? Speaking two or more languages does not cause reading difficulties or delay sound awareness. However, the milestones on this page are based on children learning English as their first language. If your child is learning English alongside another language, some tasks may look different — for example, they may find it easier to work with sounds in their stronger language first. A speech pathologist experienced with bilingual children can assess your child in a way that accounts for their full language background. Always mention your child’s language background at the start of any assessment.
P
Around age 3–4
Noticing words
Your child starts to understand that sentences are made up of separate words — for example, that “the dog barks” contains three words. This is a first step that lays the groundwork.
1
AU: Around age 5–5½
Rhyming
Your child can hear that “cat” and “hat” rhyme, and can come up with their own rhymes. Noticing rhymes comes before making them. Reading nursery rhymes and singing songs from a young age is genuinely helpful.
2
AU: Around age 4½–5
Syllables — the beats in words
Your child can clap or tap the beats in words: “but-ter-fly” = 3 beats, “dog” = 1 beat. A fun way to practise: clap your names together at dinner.
3
AU: Around age 5–5½
Same starting sounds
Your child notices that “ball,” “bat,” and “bin” all start with the same sound (/b/). Books with lots of repeated sounds (like Dr. Seuss) support this naturally.
↓ Now entering the most important zone for reading: The skills below — hearing and working with individual sounds inside words — are the strongest predictors of how well a child will learn to read.
4
AU: Around age 5–5½
Hearing the first sound in a word
“What’s the first sound in ‘sun’?” → /s/. This is often the first time a child is asked to think about individual sounds (called phonemes). This is usually where structured sound activities begin.
5
~Age 5–6
Finding the same sound across words / odd one out
“What sound is the same in bat, ball, big?” → /b/. Or: “bat, ball, cat — which doesn’t belong?” → cat. These sound like games — and they are — but they’re also important early literacy skills.
6
~Age 5–6
Blending sounds into a word
“What word is /d/–/o/–/g/?” → “dog”. This is what a child does when they “sound out” a word. It tends to be easier than pulling a word apart into sounds.
7
AU: Around age 6½–7
Breaking a word into all its sounds
“Say each sound in ‘ship’” → /sh/–/i/–/p/ (3 sounds, not 4 letters — “sh” counts as one sound). This directly supports spelling. Note: Australian children typically reach this milestone around age 6½ — a full year later than British children. This is completely normal.
8
~Age 6–7
Removing a sound from a word
“Say ‘clap’ without the /k/ sound” → “lap”. Your child can now mentally rearrange sounds, not just hear them.
9
~Age 6–8
Swapping sounds in words
“Change the /b/ in ‘bat’ to /h/” → “hat”. These are the most advanced sound tasks — and research shows they are most strongly linked to strong reading ability.
What if my child is behind? If your child is significantly behind these milestones, it doesn’t mean they won’t learn to read — it means they may benefit from targeted support. A speech pathologist can assess exactly which skills need attention. Early support makes a big difference.

These terms are frequently conflated in clinical and educational settings. Here is how they differ — and how they interact.

Phonological / Phonemic Awareness
Ears only — no letters

Definitionally auditory — no print. The continuum runs from least complex (rhyming, alliteration, sentence segmentation) through syllables and onset–rime to phoneme-level tasks (Chard & Dickson, 1999). Phonemic awareness is the phoneme-level subset. Integrating letters once letter learning is underway strengthens both skills (Ehri et al. 2001; Boyer & Ehri 2011).

🔊
Weak phonological / phonemic awareness
→ poor phonics decoding, spelling errors, difficulty with nonsense word reading, downstream reading comprehension impact.
🔤
Intact phonemic awareness, weak letter–sound knowledge
→ child can segment sounds orally but cannot map them to letters. Target explicit letter–sound teaching alongside continued PA work.

→ difficulty integrating the two systems, or insufficient explicit phonics instruction. Refer to classroom teacher or literacy specialist.

How do these skills connect to reading?
Parents often hear “phonics,” “phonological awareness,” and “letter sounds” used interchangeably — but they’re actually different things that work together.
🔊 Sound awareness (no letters)
Your child’s ability to hear and play with sounds in spoken words — before any letters are involved. It’s a purely listening skill.

Example: Clapping syllables in “bu-tter-fly”, noticing that “cat” and “hat” rhyme, or hearing that “dog” has 3 sounds: /d/ /o/ /g/.
🔤 Letter–sound knowledge
This is knowing which letter (or letters) makes which sound. For example, the letter “b” makes the /b/ sound, or “sh” together make the /sh/ sound.

This is a separate skill from sound awareness, and both need to develop — ideally around the same time once your child starts learning letters.
📖 Phonics
Phonics is when your child puts both together — using what they know about sounds AND what they know about letters to actually read and spell words.

Example: Seeing “ship” on a page and sounding it out as /sh/–/i/–/p/ → “ship”. Or hearing “ship” and writing s-h-i-p.
puzzle; How they fit together
Think of it like building a house. Sound awareness is the foundation. Letter–sound knowledge is the bricks. Phonics is putting the bricks on the foundation to build something real — reading and spelling.
What can go wrong — and what it looks like
🔊
Weak sound awareness
Your child may struggle to sound out new words, make frequent spelling mistakes, or find it hard to read unfamiliar words. This is one of the most common underlying causes of reading difficulties.
🔤
Good sound awareness, but weak letter knowledge
Your child can do sound games verbally but can’t connect those sounds to letters on a page. They may need more focused practice with letter–sound links alongside their sound work.
🗣
Speech sound difficulties
Children who have difficulty with some speech sounds (e.g. saying “wabbit” for “rabbit”) may also have difficulty hearing the difference between similar sounds. This can affect reading. Treating the speech alone, without also working on sound awareness, may not be enough to protect reading. A speech pathologist can address both together.
📖
Good sound awareness and letters, but still struggling with reading
Your child may need more explicit, structured practice connecting the two — often called systematic phonics instruction. This is typically led by classroom teachers, sometimes alongside speech pathology.
A word of reassurance: All of these difficulties are identifiable and treatable. A speech pathologist can work out exactly where the gap is and provide a targeted plan. Early identification leads to much better outcomes.

What does assessment involve?
If a speech pathologist or school recommends assessing your child’s sound awareness skills, this section explains what that typically looks like — and what tools they might use.
How a speech pathologist approaches assessment
1
Start broad
They’ll check the bigger-picture skills first — can your child clap syllables? Do they recognise rhymes? This tells them roughly where your child is starting from.
2
Zoom in on individual sounds
Then they’ll look closely at whether your child can work with the smallest sounds in words — can they blend sounds? Pull a word apart into sounds? Remove or swap a sound?
3
Check letter knowledge separately
Letter knowledge is assessed as a separate skill — not the same as sound awareness. A preschool child may have great sound awareness but no letter knowledge yet, which is completely normal.
4
If school-age: look at reading and spelling too
For children already in school, the SLP will also look at how these sound skills connect to actual reading and spelling performance, often in collaboration with the class teacher.
Tests your child might do

These are all done as spoken activities — your child doesn’t need to read or write. They’re designed to feel like a game, particularly for young children.

PIPA
For children aged 3–7 · Australian & UK norms available
This is the main tool used for younger children. It includes six short activities testing rhyming, syllable counting, identifying sounds at the start of words, and breaking words into all their sounds. It also checks letter knowledge. Takes about 20 minutes. The Australian version is developed to match the Australian school starting age, so results are directly relevant to Australian children.
CTOPP-2
For children aged 4 through to adults
A more comprehensive test used for older children or when a thorough assessment is needed. It tests sound awareness, the ability to remember sound sequences (like repeating back nonsense words), and rapid naming. Note: the comparison scores are based on American children, so your speech pathologist will interpret results with that in mind.
SPAT-R
For children in Years 1–4 · Australian norms
An Australian test commonly used once your child is at school. Rather than comparing your child to others the same age, it compares them to typical performance for their school year level. This shows not just whether there’s a gap, but which specific skills are affected — helping the speech pathologist know exactly what to target.
PAST
For children of various ages · Available free online
A free screening tool that checks whether your child can do these tasks accurately and quickly — because speed matters for reading fluency. Rather than comparing to age peers, it checks whether your child meets a standard. It’s often used alongside the SPAT-R.
What to expect: Assessment activities feel like games — listening, copying sounds, answering questions. Your child won’t need to read or write. Afterwards, your speech pathologist will explain what they found and what it means for your child’s learning.
If you’re concerned about your child — what to do next
1
Talk to your child’s teacher
Start here. Your child’s teacher sees them every day and can tell you whether they’re keeping up with reading and spelling expectations at school. Ask specifically: “How is my child going with reading and sounding out new words?” and “Have you noticed any difficulties with letter sounds or spelling?”
2
Ask for a referral to a speech pathologist
Your GP, paediatrician, or school can refer you to a speech pathologist. You can also self-refer — you don’t need a doctor’s referral to book an appointment with a private speech pathologist. If cost is a concern, ask your GP about a Chronic Disease Management plan, which may provide Medicare-subsidised sessions.
3
What to bring to the first appointment
It helps to bring: any previous reports (school, hearing, psychological), a list of your concerns and when you first noticed them, information about your child’s language background (what languages are spoken at home), and any reading or writing samples if your child is school-age. The more context the speech pathologist has, the more targeted the assessment can be.
4
After the assessment — what happens next
The speech pathologist will explain what they found and recommend next steps. This might include: therapy sessions targeting specific sound awareness skills, strategies and activities to use at home, a letter to share with your child’s teacher, or referral to another professional (e.g. educational psychologist, audiologist). You are entitled to ask questions and to understand the reasoning behind any recommendations.
Don’t wait and see. Research consistently shows that early identification and intervention leads to significantly better outcomes. If your child is in preschool or prep and you have concerns, it is not too early to seek an assessment. If your child is already at school and struggling, it is not too late — intervention at any age can make a meaningful difference.

A practical guide to intervention target selection, sequencing, and SLP scope considerations.

Intervention target decision guide

PA — Sub-phoneme levels

Start here for preschool/prep, or if the child fails syllable, rhyme or alliteration tasks. These are the least complex end of the PA continuum (Chard & Dickson, 1999) — important foundations, but don’t linger. Phoneme-level work drives literacy outcomes most directly.

  • Rhyme detection before rhyme production (detection is passive, production is active)
  • Alliteration awareness — first-sound recognition across words
  • Syllable clapping, counting, blending, deleting
  • Compound word manipulation as entry to deletion (“football” → “foot”)
  • Onset–rime blending and segmentation as bridge to phoneme level

Phonemic awareness

Sequence: isolation → identity/categorisation → blending + segmentation (integrated) → deletion → manipulation.

  • Minimal pair tasks (bat/pat, cup/pup)
  • Elkonin / sound boxes for segmentation
  • Integrated blending + segmentation — not one then the other
  • Progress to deletion and substitution for stronger reading gains

Letter–sound knowledge

Teach alongside phonemic awareness once letter learning is underway. Combined instruction more effective than either alone (Ehri et al. 2001, NRP).

  • Explicit letter name + sound pairing
  • Sound sorts, initial sound picture matching
  • Letter tracing with simultaneous vocalisation

Phonics (school age)

Use connected phonation (“mmmaaannn” not “m–a–n”) — more effective for CVC decoding (Gonzalez-Frey & Ehri 2020). SLPs are well-placed to deliver phonics-embedded intervention, particularly for children with SSD, DLD, or dyslexia (SPA, 2024). Collaboration with classroom teachers at the Tier 1 level remains important.

  • Decodable texts matched to current letter knowledge
  • Explicit GPC instruction + blending practice
  • Word-building with tiles / letter manipulatives
Evidence base for sequencing
✓
Integrated blending + segmentation
Produces greater literacy benefit than targeting either in isolation (NRP 2000). Blending is a reasonable starting point, but both should be taught together.
✓
Manipulation tasks predict reading most strongly
More so than segmentation and blending alone (Kilpatrick 2012, 2015; see also Catts et al. 2001). Progress to deletion and substitution once blending and segmentation are established.
✓
PA instruction combined with letters
More effective than PA instruction alone for reading outcomes (Ehri et al. 2001, NRP meta-analysis; Boyer & Ehri 2011).
✓
Resolving SSD without targeting PA
Has little impact on literacy outcomes. PA intervention group in Gillon (2000) outperformed the speech-only group on reading and spelling despite similar gains in speech production.
✓
Rhyme and syllable instruction alone
Without progressing to phoneme-level work does not directly improve reading acquisition (NRP 2000). Target phoneme level as priority for literacy outcomes.
SLP scope note (SPA 2024): Speech pathologists assess, diagnose, and intervene across the full literacy continuum — including phonological awareness, phonemic awareness, letter–sound knowledge, and phonics-embedded intervention. This is explicit in SPA’s 2024 Clinical Guidelines and position statements. SLPs in school settings are well-placed to deliver phonics-embedded intervention, particularly for children with SSD, DLD, or dyslexia — and SPA has advocated for SLP involvement in systematic phonics-based approaches nationally. Close collaboration with classroom teachers and literacy specialists remains essential, particularly for Tier 1 classroom instruction, but collaborative practice does not limit SLP scope.

How does therapy actually help?
If your child is receiving speech pathology support for sound awareness, this section explains what that typically involves — and what you can do at home to support their progress.
What therapy looks like at each stage
1
Starting with bigger sounds — for preschool and prep
If your child is in preschool or prep, or they’re struggling with rhymes and syllables, therapy often starts here. Activities might include:

→ Rhyme games — “does ‘cat’ rhyme with ‘hat’?”
→ Clapping or tapping syllables in words and names
→ Listening games with songs, books, and made-up words

The goal is to build a strong ear for how words are structured before zooming in on individual sounds.
2
Working with individual sounds — the most important stage
This is usually the core of therapy for children with reading difficulties. Activities build gradually:

→ Identifying the first sound in a word (“what sound does ‘sun’ start with?”)
→ Sound boxes (Elkonin boxes) — your child pushes a counter for each sound they hear
→ Blending sounds: /d/–/o/–/g/ → “dog”
→ Breaking words apart: “ship” → /sh/–/i/–/p/
→ Removing and swapping sounds (the most advanced — and most powerful — tasks)

Note about letter cards: Once your child is learning their letters, the speech pathologist will often bring letter cards into these activities — research shows this combination is more effective than sound work alone.
3
Connecting sounds to letters
Once your child is starting to learn their letters (usually at school), therapy may include explicitly connecting each sound to its letter. For example:

→ Saying the sound while tracing the letter
→ Sorting pictures by their starting sound and matching to a letter
→ Letter sound pairs: “b says /b/, d says /d/”

This isn’t reading yet — it’s building the bridge between what they hear and what they see on the page.
4
Phonics and reading — for school-age children
For school-age children, the speech pathologist may work on applying sound skills directly to reading and spelling. One key technique:

Connected phonation: When sounding out a word like “man”, saying “mmmaaannn” (sounds stretched and joined) rather than “m – a – n” (sounds chopped up). Research shows the joined-up version helps children read words much more easily.

Note: structured phonics teaching is usually led by classroom teachers. The speech pathologist’s role is to make sure the underlying sound skills are strong enough to support it.
What you can do at home: Everyday activities make a real difference — rhyming books, tongue twisters, “I spy” using sounds (not letters), clapping syllables in names, and reading aloud regularly. Your speech pathologist will give you specific activities matched to where your child is up to. Little and often is more effective than one long session a week.
A note about roles: Speech pathologists focus on the sound awareness skills that underpin reading. Classroom teachers lead the phonics and reading instruction. When both work together and communicate regularly, children make faster progress. If your child has both, it’s worth asking for a conversation between them.
